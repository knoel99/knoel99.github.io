Official abstract and objectives

The abstract published by the ANR starts from a physical observation. The dynamo effect, that is, the amplification of a magnetic field by the flow of a conducting fluid, is hard to reproduce: only three experiments, including the one at Cadarache, have achieved it with liquid sodium, and the DRESDYN experiment, in Germany, seeks to achieve it with a precessing cylinder. Direct numerical simulations are very costly, which motivates reduced models and machine learning, with an announced difficulty: capturing rare events. The abstract adds that “data assimilation (DA) with ML is becoming more accessible, notably thanks to the TorchDA platform” (our translation), S. Cheng’s software.

The two objectives are stated as follows (excerpt from the French abstract on the ANR page, in our translation):

“MilaDy aims to advance MHD research with two main objectives: (i) Study magnetic field reversals and ‘explosive’ dynamo phenomena using data-driven methods to identify key features and develop ROMs that reproduce the observed dynamics. The project investigates whether ML can improve the understanding of magnetic reversals and create faster models that identify phases such as the precursor, the reversal and the recovery. (ii) Optimize the DRESDYN device with parametric ROMs to explore the conditions of DyE, such as precession/rotation frequency ratios. ROMs allow efficient parameter exploration, helping experimentalists manage these complex systems.”

In this excerpt, “DyE” denotes the dynamo effect and “ROM” a reduced-order model.

Objective (i): reversals and explosive dynamos. A reversal is the sign change of the magnetic dipole, as in the reversals of the Earth’s field; the VKS experiment at Cadarache produced some in the laboratory (Berhanu et al. 2007). The English version of the abstract speaks of “burst dynamo phenomena” and of models able to distinguish the “precursor, reversal, and recovery” phases. The aim is thus to build, from the simulations, reduced models that reproduce this dynamics and recognize its stages.

Objective (ii): DRESDYN. DRESDYN is a precession-driven dynamo experiment conducted at the HZDR research center in Dresden. The project intends to contribute to it with parametric reduced models, which make it possible to explore at low cost the conditions for dynamo onset as a function of the ratio between the precession frequency and the rotation frequency.

First affiliated publications

On November 6, 2026, a HAL search for the reference ANR-25-CE51-0077 returns three records. The first two are at the heart of the project.

Bousquet, Ponty, Botez, Plihon and Nore (2026), Physical Review Fluids 11, 063703. The paper studies field reversals in simulations of the VKS experiment, with two solvers: LATU, a pseudo-spectral code with penalization (Kreuzahler et al. 2017), and SFEMaNS (Nore et al. 2018). The reference simulation is at Re = 1594 and Rm = 531, with a forcing asymmetry of 0.058 and a relative permeability \mu_r = 16 in the impellers; a conducting outer medium proved necessary for reversals in the explored range. A proper orthogonal decomposition (POD) on 2000 snapshots reveals four dominant axisymmetric modes, and a reduced Galerkin model, called DQVU, is identified by constrained regression with PySINDy; it reproduces the asymmetric bimodal distribution of the amplitude V of the zonal mode, which the earlier DQV model did not. The data are not public: they are available from the authors upon reasonable request.

Botez, Giesecke, Nore, Cappanera and Stefani (2026), Physical Review Fluids 11, 083701 (published August 14, 2026). The paper computes the kinematic dynamo generated by averaged velocity fields in a precessing cylinder, within the narrow window of Poincaré numbers where the dynamo is most likely. It shows that the simplified boundary conditions with vanishing tangential field systematically underestimate the threshold Rm_c, and that conducting or permeable walls can favor or suppress the dynamo. This is a direct application to DRESDYN.

The third record (Barré and Horii 2026) is a mathematical work on large deviations, which also acknowledges the project; its link with the internship is weak. As of the same date, no publication by S. Cheng or D. Lucor on dynamos was found on arXiv or HAL.

How the 2027 internship fits in

The 2026 internship learned to represent dynamo fields. The 2027 internship moves on to generation. Its offer states the objective in one sentence: “design and evaluate physics-constrained generative models for parameter-dependent physical fields in dynamo systems”. The generator must respect “energy balance and divergence-free conditions”, encode variations with respect to the control parameters (“Reynolds number, magnetic Reynolds number, material properties…”) and produce “3D fields that can serve as input for reduced-order models or data assimilation frameworks developed elsewhere in the project”.

The requested work follows a precise order: conditional models by diffusion or flow matching, constraints derived from the equations and the boundary conditions, validation on simple PDEs, then evaluation of MHD fields with physical metrics (“energy spectra, helicity, divergence control, and topology preservation”). The deliverables are a comparative study of constraint strategies, a technical report and code integrated into the “MilaDy project toolbox”. The offer recommends two readings, FunDiff (Wang et al. 2026) and Fukami and Taira (2023), and announces a possible funded PhD depending on the results of the internship.

The internship takes place mainly at CEREA, under the supervision of S. Cheng (ENPC) and D. Lucor (LISN), in collaboration with C. Nore (LISN) and Y. Ponty (OCA). S. Cheng’s “Opportunities” page specifies that the PhD would be co-supervised by S. Cheng and D. Lucor.

Key point The 2026 internship learned to represent dynamo fields; the 2027 internship learns to generate them, conditioned on the parameters and under physical constraints, to feed the project’s reduced models and data assimilation.

Sibo Cheng

Sibo Cheng has held a junior professor chair (a tenure-track position) at the École nationale des ponts et chaussées (Institut Polytechnique de Paris), at CEREA, since 2024; he remains an Honorary Research Fellow at Imperial College London. He was previously a researcher at the Data Science Institute of Imperial College (2020–2024), with Rossella Arcucci. He defended his PhD at Université Paris-Saclay in 2020, under the supervision of D. Lucor, on the specification of error covariances in data assimilation, in collaboration with EDF R&D.

His stated research themes are data assimilation and inverse problems, reduced-order and latent-space modeling, scientific machine learning (networks coupled with physical models, differentiable simulation, neural operators) and generative modeling, which he describes as probabilistic forecasting and field reconstruction with conditional generative models. His applications range from wildfires to air pollution and geophysical systems. He holds a Hi! PARIS grant (2026–2030) and leads the ANR JCJC project OFML (wildfires), and he is a co-investigator of MilaDy and of the QWERTY project (PEPR FORESTT).

He has developed two software packages: TorchDA, a data assimilation library with deep networks, cited in the MilaDy abstract and directly useful to the internship, and PyTorchFire, a differentiable cellular automaton for wildfire spread. Several of his PhD students work on generative modeling or on representation for assimilation, including Lisa Achard (multimodal generative modeling and uncertainty quantification), supervised mainly by D. Lucor and Zeyu Fu. His research page summarizes the role of CEREA in MilaDy as follows: “physics-aware representation learning and reduced-order modelling of three-dimensional velocity and magnetic fields, connecting learned representations to data assimilation”.

Didier Lucor is a CNRS research director (directeur de recherche) at LISN, where he belongs to the DATAFLOT group (data science, transition, instabilities, control and turbulence) of the mechanics department; he was deputy director of the laboratory from 2022 to 2025. Caroline Nore

Caroline Nore is referred to as a professor in the project’s offers (“Pr. Caroline Nore (LISN)” in the internship offer, “Pr. UPS” in the postdoctoral offer). She works at LISN, in the COMET team according to the HAL records of her recent papers, and she coordinates MilaDy. Her research concerns computational magnetohydrodynamics and the dynamo effect: simulation of the VKS experiment, precession dynamos, the Taylor–Couette dynamo. Her other topics, such as liquid metal batteries or ferrofluids, are further removed from the internship.

Her main tool is SFEMaNS, which the M2 internship offer she proposed with D. Lucor calls “our massively parallel research code SFEMaNS”; she co-authored with J.-L. Guermond the method papers on which it is based (Guermond et al. 2007, 2009, 2011). The code solves the Navier–Stokes equations, the induction equation and the full MHD coupling in cylindrical coordinates, with a Fourier decomposition in the azimuthal direction and Lagrange finite elements in the meridian plane. It handles jumps in magnetic permeability, the matching to a scalar potential in the vacuum and, for large Reynolds numbers, stabilization by entropy viscosity. For learning, the consequence is direct: the data are Fourier modes on an unstructured mesh, not arrays on a Cartesian grid.

She has supervised PhDs on MHD and the dynamo (F. Luddens in 2012 with J.-L. Guermond, L. Cappanera in 2015, M. Creff in 2023 with B. Dubrulle) and co-supervised a PhD in machine learning, that of M. Quattromini (2024), on graph neural networks for data assimilation and optimization in fluid mechanics.

Yannick Ponty

Yannick Ponty has been a CNRS research director (directeur de recherche) since 2014, at the Lagrange laboratory (UMR 7293) of the Observatoire de la Côte d’Azur, in Nice. He has led the “Turbulence Fluide et Plasma” (fluid and plasma turbulence) team since October 2025, having already led it from 2011 to 2021, and he is an external member of the Inria CASTOR team. He was a CNRS research scientist (chargé de recherche) from 2000 to 2014 and a research fellow in the MHD group of the University of Exeter from 1997 to 2000. His PhD (1997) dealt with rotating convective patterns and the dynamo effect in a chaotic flow, and his habilitation (2012) with the role of turbulence in the dynamo effect and in magnetic induction.

His stated keywords are magnetohydrodynamics, the dynamo effect, turbulence, high-performance computing and optimal control. In Bochum, he co-supervised with R. Grauer the PhD of S. Kreuzahler on the penalization of the VKS impellers in a pseudo-spectral code; Bousquet et al. (2026) use the name “LATU” for the solver of Kreuzahler et al. (2017) that came out of this work. He has supervised a MilaDy postdoc since February 2026 and supervised an M1 internship on high-performance computing with Kokkos in 2025.

His contribution to the internship does not lie in machine learning, a field in which we found no publication of his. It lies in the physics of dynamos at low magnetic Prandtl number, intermittency and rare events, subcritical bifurcations, pseudo-spectral codes and the high-order statistics used to validate generated fields.

Candidate PhD directions

The six directions below are proposals written for this site on the basis of the checked sources. Their key challenges and risks are inferred from these sources; they were not formulated as such by the supervisors. Each direction is described by its statement, the scientific challenge it tackles, the work it builds on, the data it depends on and its risks. In SFEMaNS and in Bousquet et al. (2026), the magnetic field is denoted \mathbf{H}; in a domain with variable permeability, the divergence constraint then applies to \mu_r\mathbf{H}.

Direction A — Conditional generator of parametric 3D MHD fields

Statement. Direction A learns the conditional distribution p(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{H} \mid Re, Rm, \mu_r, \delta) of the VKS velocity and magnetic fields, where \delta denotes the forcing asymmetry between the two impellers. The generator is a diffusion or flow matching model in a latent space. The autoencoder that defines this space is constrained by the symmetries of the flow (rotation R_\pi, equivariance between the latent and physical spaces), regressed on the parameters and possibly aligned with the POD modes.

Challenge. The model must generalize beyond the few points (Re, Rm, \mu_r) actually simulated, preserve \nabla\cdot\mathbf{u} = 0 and \nabla\cdot(\mu_r\mathbf{H}) = 0 after decoding, preserve the symmetries, and handle a hybrid representation made of azimuthal Fourier modes and unstructured finite elements.

Builds on. The direction extends the interpretable autoencoders of Bousquet, Nore and Lucor (2025), the VKS analyses of Bousquet et al. (2024), the latent regression and contrastive learning of the 2026 internship, the conditional diffusion of Zhuang, Cheng and Duraisamy (2025), the multi-fidelity constraints of Zhou, Cheng and Arcucci (2024) and the latent surrogate models of Zhou et al. (2026, preprint).

Data. It relies on the SFEMaNS simulations of VKS (Nore et al. 2016, 2018, with sweeps in permeability and in Reynolds number; Creff et al. 2024, with steel or soft-iron impellers) and on those of LATU (Kreuzahler et al. 2017, relative permeability from 1 to 16).

Risks. The parametric points are few, because each MHD simulation is costly, as the ANR abstract stresses. The volume of the 3D fields requires working in a latent space. The data are not public and must be obtained through the consortium. Finally, the tension between reconstruction and regularization already observed for VAEs (Bousquet et al. 2025) may limit the quality of the latent space.

Direction B — Magnetic reversals and rare events through probabilistic generation

Statement. Direction B builds a temporal generative model, for example an autoregressive diffusion or a diffusion conditioned on the current state, which produces ensembles of field trajectories in the vicinity of reversals. Sampling is guided by the DQVU reduced model or by Galerkin constraints, in the manner of universal guidance (Bansal et al. 2024). The goal is to estimate reversal probabilities, waiting times and the precursor, reversal and recovery phases targeted by objective (i).

Challenge. Reversals are rare (a simulation contains few of them), their statistics have heavy tails (power laws for the active phases), and the generated 3D field must remain consistent with the reduced dynamics while staying calibrated in the probabilistic sense.

Builds on. The direction extends the analysis of reversals and the DQVU model of Bousquet et al. (2026), the on-off intermittency of Alexakis and Ponty (2008), the subcritical bifurcations of Ponty et al. (2007) and of Mannix, Ponty and Marcotte (2022), the aperiodic Taylor–Couette reversals (Nore et al. 2012), the diffusion ensembles of Yu et al. (2026) and the invariant-measure-preserving learning of Cheng X. et al. (2025).

Data. It uses the reversal simulations of Bousquet et al. (2026), at Re = 1594, Rm = 531 and \mu_r = 16 with an outer conducting layer, and Y. Ponty’s simulations of ABC and Taylor–Green dynamos for intermittency.

Risks. Long time series are needed, hence substantial computation. The DQVU model reproduces only imperfectly the distribution of the amplitude Q of the quadrupolar mode (Bousquet et al. 2026). Finally, rare-event metrics are hard to validate statistically with few events.

Direction C — Data assimilation with a generative prior

Statement. Direction C reconstructs 3D states (\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{H}) from synthetic sparse observations, for example simulated magnetic probes, by combining a generative prior (conditional diffusion) with variational or ensemble assimilation in the latent space.

Challenge. The posterior distribution is multimodal, since the dipole can have either polarity, and therefore non-Gaussian. The observation operator is nonlinear, the dimension is large, and the analysis must remain physically consistent.

Builds on. The direction extends generalized latent assimilation (Cheng et al. 2022), the learned inverse operator VIVID (Cheng et al. 2024), TorchDA (Cheng et al. 2025), the 4D-Var with generative dynamics of Bocquet, Finn and Cheng (2026, preprint), the review by Cheng et al. (2023) and the conformal coverage of Jaber et al. (2025).

Data. It uses subsampled SFEMaNS and LATU outputs, and intermediate test beds (Lorenz, shallow water) already available in TorchDA.

Risks. No experimental data from VKS or DRESDYN has been identified as accessible: one would probably have to work with synthetic observations. The cost of diffusion sampling at each assimilation cycle may also be prohibitive.

Direction D — Parametric generative model to explore DRESDYN

Statement. Direction D generates mean or fluctuating velocity fields conditioned on the Poincaré number, the Reynolds number and the wall properties, then estimates the threshold Rm_c and the symmetry of the dominant mode. The estimate is obtained with a kinematic solver applied to the generated fields, or with a prediction head, within a robust Bayesian optimization framework.

Challenge. The window of Poincaré numbers where the dynamo is likely is narrow (Botez et al. 2026), the transitions between hydrodynamic states are abrupt, and Rm_c is very sensitive to the electromagnetic conditions (vacuum or pseudo-vacuum, conducting or permeable walls).

Builds on. The direction extends Botez et al. (2026), the precession dynamos of Nore et al. (2011) and Cappanera et al. (2016), the preparatory work for DRESDYN (Stefani et al. 2015; Giesecke et al. 2018), the robust Bayesian optimization of Zhou, Wang, Lucor and Sauret (2026) and the latent variational calibration of Zhou et al. (2026, preprint).

Data. It depends on the hydrodynamic precession simulations and the averaged fields of Botez et al., computed with SFEMaNS in collaboration with HZDR. Objective (ii) of the ANR explicitly calls for parametric reduced models for DRESDYN.

Risks. The simulated configurations are few. The generator’s error propagates into a sensitive eigenvalue problem. Finally, the direction depends on the DRESDYN schedule; the ANR abstract describes DRESDYN as an “ongoing” experiment, and the postdoc offer calls its success uncertain.

Direction E — Hard constraints on hybrid Fourier–finite element meshes

Statement. Direction E formulates diffusion or flow matching in a function space, in the manner of FunDiff (Wang et al. 2026), or directly on the SFEMaNS representation (azimuthal modes on meridian finite elements). It imposes exactly \nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0 (through a vector potential or a poloidal–toroidal decomposition), the interface conditions at permeability jumps and the matching to the vacuum, and it controls the energy budget by projection at sampling time or through a loss.

Challenge. The meshes are unstructured, the permeability is discontinuous (continuity of the tangential component of \mathbf{H} and of the normal component of \mu\mathbf{H}), the impellers are penalized zones, and a hard constraint can degrade the quality of the samples.

Builds on. The direction extends the numerical methods of SFEMaNS (Guermond et al. 2007, 2009, 2011), the study of the role of boundary conditions by Giesecke et al. (2012), the review by Cheng et al. (2025) on unstructured meshes, the moment constraints of Xue et al. (2026) and the relaxation of incompressibility by Lucor, Agrawal and Sergent (2022).

Data. It works on native SFEMaNS outputs, after validation on simple PDEs, as the offer requires.

Risks. The implementation on an unstructured mesh is costly, hard constraints can reduce the diversity of the samples, and the integration of a generator written in PyTorch with a massively parallel simulation code is not a given.

Direction F — Test beds and physical metrics for MHD generators

Statement. Direction F builds a ladder of test beds, from simple PDEs to periodic dynamos (Taylor–Green, ABC, Roberts) computed with Y. Ponty’s codes, then to VKS and precession with SFEMaNS and LATU. It pairs this ladder with a battery of metrics: spectra (including the Kazantsev spectrum), helicity, divergence, topology, local energy transfers (Creff et al. 2024), kurtosis–skewness relations (Ponty et al. 2025), conformal coverage (Jaber et al. 2025) and the “hit rate” score (Yu et al. 2026).

Challenge. The metrics must truly discriminate between models and must not depend on the mesh, and 3D diagnostics are expensive.

Builds on. The direction extends the Taylor–Green dynamos of Ponty et al. (2005, 2007), the transition studied by Ponty and Plunian (2011), the statistics of Ponty, Politano and Pouquet (2025), the budgets of Creff et al. (2024) and the metrics of the 2026 internship.

Data. Periodic simulations in a Cartesian box are simpler to produce and to learn, and long series exist (up to 3.14\times10^{5} turnover times). The public dataset The Well (Ohana et al. 2024) also contains 3D MHD turbulence, but compressible and without dynamo effect: it can serve as a test bed for learning, not as a substitute for dynamo data.

Risks. The topic is mostly methodological, and a test bed risks being judged incremental. It is better to attach it to direction A or direction B.

The French title of the topic uses the word renversements, the usual term in the dynamo literature, whereas the ANR abstract uses inversions; both are rendered here as “reversals” and denote the sign change of the magnetic dipole. The detailed topic, with its theoretical part, its experimental plan and its timeline, is presented in the PhD proposal; the preliminary results of this site’s toy experiments are presented in the internship report, written in advance since the internship has not yet taken place.

Why merge A and B

The first reason is the ranking: A and B are the two best directions under all three sets of weights tried. The second is their complementarity. A answers the offer and provides the parametric exploration method targeted by objective (ii); B answers objective (i), centered on reversals. A PhD that combines them therefore covers both what the internship asks for and what the project promised. If the merged direction is scored with the same grid, it inherits the best scores of each for the offer, the ANR, novelty and coverage of the supervisors (5 everywhere), keeps the scores of A for data access and risk (4 and 3), since a snapshot generator is sufficient (see below), and loses one point of feasibility compared with A (3), because its scope is broader; its weighted score would then be 4.45. This figure is favorable by construction and should not be over-interpreted.

The third reason is the most important: a single theoretical thread links the two directions and removes the main risk of B. That risk was the need for long time series to learn the dynamics of reversals. Yet, as we shall see, a generator of snapshots, that is, the object of direction A, is enough to predict the mean frequency of the dipole’s zero crossings, hence of reversals up to excursions, without a temporal model. Validation still requires reference series, and the training snapshots must cover the rare region where the dipole is close to zero; but training no longer requires time-resolved trajectories.

The theoretical thread: hard constraints, distributional constraints and Rice’s formula

The theoretical framework prepared for the internship distinguishes two families of constraints. Hard constraints apply to each sample: zero divergence, linear boundary conditions. They are imposed by construction, for example by generating a vector potential \mathbf{A} and setting \mathbf{B} = \nabla\times\mathbf{A}, or by projection. Symmetries occupy an intermediate position: they concern the distribution (a field and its image under the symmetry, for example \mathbf{B} and -\mathbf{B}, must be equally probable), but an equivariant network imposes them exactly.

Distributional constraints apply to the distribution of the samples, not to each of them. Let \dot{\mathbf{x}} = F_{\boldsymbol\lambda}(\mathbf{x}) be the discretized dynamics at fixed parameters \boldsymbol\lambda, and \mu_{\boldsymbol\lambda} the distribution of its states in the statistically stationary regime. For every smooth test function \varphi with controlled growth, stationarity imposes

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mu_{\boldsymbol\lambda}}\big[F_{\boldsymbol\lambda}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\nabla\varphi(\mathbf{x})\big] = 0 .

With \varphi = E, the energy, we recover the mean budget: on average, the injected power equals the dissipated power. An isolated snapshot does not satisfy this budget in general, since the time derivative of the energy is not zero at every instant; it is a constraint on the distribution, which can be imposed through a loss during training, including at parameters for which no data are available, or through exponential tilting at sampling time. The offer asks for the energy balance to be respected: this formulation states in what precise sense this can be done.

Rice’s formula (Rice 1944) makes the link with reversals. Let D = \ell(\mathbf{x}) be the dipole moment, a linear observable of the state. For a stationary process with smooth trajectories, whose pair (D, \dot D) has a continuous joint density, the mean number of zero crossings of D per unit time is

\nu_0 = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mu_{\boldsymbol\lambda}}\Big[\,\big|\ell\big(F_{\boldsymbol\lambda}(\mathbf{x})\big)\big|\;\delta\big(\ell(\mathbf{x})\big)\Big] .

This quantity depends only on the stationary distribution and on F_{\boldsymbol\lambda}. In MHD, the time derivative of the dipole can be computed on a snapshot (\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{B}) thanks to the induction equation. A snapshot generator conditioned on \boldsymbol\lambda therefore predicts the reversal frequency as a function of the parameters, without time integration. The formula has limitations that must be stated: it requires knowing F_{\boldsymbol\lambda}, it also counts quick crossings back and forth around zero (excursions), and it does not give the distribution of waiting times between reversals.

Finally, the dynamo threshold raises a difficulty of its own. Suppose that the distribution of states jumps at \lambda_c: on either side of the threshold, the distributions \mu_\lambda remain at a Wasserstein distance W_1 of at least J > 0 from each other, as in a subcritical bifurcation with hysteresis (Ponty et al. 2007). A generator whose dependence on \lambda is K-Lipschitz in the W_1 sense, that is, W_1(q_\lambda, q_{\lambda'}) \le K|\lambda - \lambda'|, then satisfies, for every half-width r > 0,

\max_{|\lambda-\lambda_c|\le r} W_1\big(q_\lambda, \mu_\lambda\big) \;\ge\; \frac{J - 2Kr}{2} .

The proof is a single triangle inequality between \mu_{\lambda_c - r}, q_{\lambda_c - r}, q_{\lambda_c + r} and \mu_{\lambda_c + r}. A smooth generator therefore makes an error of at least J/2 near the jump, unless one accepts a large constant K or also conditions on the state. For a supercritical bifurcation, the distribution varies continuously but with an infinite slope at the threshold, and the difficulty persists in a milder form. These statements and their proofs are developed in the internship report and in the PhD proposal.

Continuity with the internship and reuse of the toolbox

The internship must produce a comparative study of constraint strategies (hard, soft, distributional). The preparatory experiments in this repository (experiences/ folder, small models run on CPU) already lay the groundwork on three test beds: a three-mode reversal model (Gissinger 2012), a forced 2D MHD system, and a 3D kinematic ABC dynamo. The PhD would reuse the same code, integrated into the project’s toolbox as the offer requires, and would transfer it to the consortium’s 3D data: first LATU, whose Cartesian grid is the simplest, then SFEMaNS and its hybrid mesh.

The generated fields directly feed the project’s existing tools. They can serve as input to the reduced models (POD and the DQVU model of Bousquet et al. 2026, autoencoders of Bousquet, Nore and Lucor 2025) and to the assimilation frameworks (TorchDA), which matches the sentence of the offer about “input for reduced-order models or data assimilation frameworks”. Finally, let us note a connection, which reflects our own reading and not that of the authors: the regression that identifies DQVU already imposes a statistical constraint, the conservation of a third-order moment (Bousquet et al. 2026). A constraint on averages, rather than on each instant, is therefore already used in the consortium, in another setting.

Third-year extensions: directions C and D

Once the conditional generator is established, two extensions become natural. Toward direction C, the generator serves as a prior in latent assimilation (generalized latent assimilation, TorchDA, 4D-Var with generative dynamics) to reconstruct (\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{B}) from synthetic magnetic probes; the posterior distribution is then bimodal, because of the two polarities of the dipole, and a prior that respects the symmetry \mathbf{B} \to -\mathbf{B}, and hence gives the same weight to both polarities, avoids biasing the analysis toward one of them. Toward direction D, the generator is conditioned on the Poincaré number to estimate Rm_c and the symmetry of the dominant mode in the DRESDYN geometry, relying on the simulations of Botez et al. (2026) if they are accessible. The choice between the two will depend on the available data and on the progress made in the first two years.

What is already published and what remains to be done

Several building blocks of the selected direction are already published; they must be cited, not claimed.

  • The characterization of an invariant distribution by the identities \mathbb{E}_\mu[\mathcal{L}\varphi] = 0, where \mathcal{L} is the generator of the dynamics, is classical (Echeverría 1982; Ethier and Kurtz 1986), and several works learn a dynamics through its invariant measure, for example DySLIM (Schiff et al. 2024).
  • The weak form of the stationary Fokker–Planck equation has already been used as a loss to train a sample generator: this is the Weak Generative Sampler (Cai, Cao, Huang and Zhou 2026), tested up to dimension 100 with random Gaussian test functions.
  • Fine-tuning a flow matching model toward a distribution tilted by a weak-form PDE residual has been published (Tauberschmidt, Fellenz, Vollmer and Duncan, ICLR 2026); the residual there is, however, evaluated on each sample.
  • Zero divergence is imposed exactly through a stream function in 2D in FunDiff (Wang et al. 2026). Divergence-free diffusion models based on Leray projection have been published in periodic 2D (Genuist, Savin, Gatti and Clouteau 2026, preprint), with a comparison of six variants concluding that no strategy dominates everywhere.
  • The same idea exists in 3D, in a periodic domain, for rotating turbulence (PCDM, Li, Buzzicotti, Bonaccorso and Biferale 2026, preprint), with zero divergence to machine precision.
  • Exponential tilting of a generator under moment constraints is also known (Khalifa et al. 2021; Mun 2026, preprint), but with external target values.

What remains to be done, to our knowledge after a literature search as of November 6, 2026, comes down to five points.

  1. No published generative model (diffusion or flow matching) has been found on dynamo simulations, whether geodynamo, VKS, DRESDYN or 3D turbulent dynamo.
  2. The stationarity identities of a field PDE (energy and cross-helicity budgets in MHD) have not been found used as distributional constraints of a parametric conditional generator, enforceable at parameters without data and without target values.
  3. The inconsistency of a per-sample constraint for a stationary budget has not been found in the literature. The internship report, which is prospective, states it and measures it on toy models; it remains to be measured on 3D dynamos.
  4. No prior work has been found for Rice’s formula applied to a snapshot generator.
  5. No generative model found jointly imposes \nabla\cdot\mathbf{u} = 0 and \nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0 in 3D, let alone in a bounded cylindrical domain with permeability jumps; none is conditioned jointly on Re and Rm, and none studies the crossing of the dynamo threshold.

These negative claims cannot be proven: they will have to be rechecked through literature monitoring throughout the PhD. Two points of caution also accompany the topic. The stationarity identities are necessary but not sufficient, because every invariant measure satisfies them, including those supported by unstable orbits: fitting to the data remains indispensable. And they do not fix the relative weights of two symmetric states, for example the two polarities of the dipole: the symmetry must be imposed separately, through the architecture.
