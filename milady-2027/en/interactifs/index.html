Interactive diagrams

Five thought experiments to manipulate in the browser

About Each diagram is a small, self-contained JavaScript program that runs in your browser, with no server. The formulas it uses are written at the top of each file (assets/interactif/<nom>.js), together with their references. These diagrams complement the static figures of the report and of the preparation modules; they do not replace the proofs.

The five diagrams follow the order of the curriculum: the transport of probability distributions that underlies flow matching, the projection that makes a field divergence-free, the difference between a per-sample constraint and a constraint in distribution, then two dynamical systems from the internship, the reversal model and the ABC kinematic dynamo.

1. Flow matching: transporting a distribution

A flow matching model transports a simple distribution, here a Gaussian, to the data distribution through a velocity field v(\mathbf{x}, t). This diagram uses the exact marginal field for a target distribution made of two symmetric Gaussians \pm\mathbf{m}(\lambda): it is the field that a network would try to learn. The slider \lambda plays the role of a physical conditioning parameter, like Rm in the internship.

Notice two things. The conditional paths \mathbf{x}_t = (1-t)\mathbf{x}_0 + t\mathbf{x}_1 are straight but cross each other; the trajectories of the marginal flow never cross. The final distribution is symmetric because the field is: this is Proposition 4 of the report at work.

To go further: module p7 — generative models, chapter on flow matching.

2. Leray projection

Every periodic vector field decomposes into a divergence-free part and a gradient part (Helmholtz–Hodge decomposition). The Leray projection \mathbb{P} removes exactly the gradient part: in Fourier space, \hat{\mathbf{u}}_{\mathbf{k}} \mapsto \hat{\mathbf{u}}_{\mathbf{k}} - \mathbf{k}(\mathbf{k}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{\mathbf{k}})/|\mathbf{k}|^2. The slider \alpha sets the compressible share of the field; the color map shows \nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}.

The projection can only remove energy (\|\mathbb{P}\mathbf{u}\|^2 \le \|\mathbf{u}\|^2). On a bounded domain, it would require solving a Poisson problem with boundary conditions: this is one of the reasons why the report prefers to generate potentials.

To go further: module p1 — vector calculus and Fourier and experiment E2.

3. Constraint in distribution or per-sample constraint

In a statistically stationary state, the energy variation r = \mathrm{d}E/\mathrm{d}t = P - \varepsilon fluctuates: it is zero only on average. This diagram compares three distributions of r: the true distribution, the distribution of a biased model in which each sample is projected onto r = 0, and the same distribution corrected by exponential tilting w_i \propto e^{\beta r_i}. The per-sample projection satisfies the constraint by destroying the variance; the tilting corrects the mean while distorting the distribution as little as possible in the Kullback–Leibler sense.

Check “missing support”: if the model has never produced the right tail of the true distribution, no reweighting can create it. The effective sample size collapses. This is what experiments E1 and E2 observe, and it is why the report instead imposes the constraint during training.

To go further: module p5 — probability and optimal transport (I-projection) and module p8 — physical constraints.

4. Dipole reversals: the Gissinger model

Gissinger’s three-mode model (2012) couples a dipolar mode D, a quadrupolar mode Q and a velocity mode V. It reproduces chaotic reversals of the dipole, analogous to those of the VKS experiment. Vary \Gamma: below about 0.865, the trajectory stays on one side (no reversal); above it, the two symmetric attractors have merged and D changes sign irregularly.

The diagram counts the zero crossings of D and compares them with the estimate given by Rice’s formula, computed from the visited states alone: \nu_0 \approx \mathbb{E}\big[|\dot D|\,\mathbf{1}_{|D|<h}\big]/(2h). This is the idea behind Proposition 8 of the report: a generative model of the stationary distribution is enough to predict the reversal frequency, without integrating in time.

To go further: module p4 — dynamical systems and experiment E1.

5. ABC kinematic dynamo

The ABC flow (A = B = C = 1) is a kinematic dynamo: for some values of the magnetic Reynolds number Rm = 1/\eta, an initially weak magnetic field grows exponentially. The curve shows the growth rate \lambda(Rm) computed in this repository on a 32^3 grid; the shaded areas are the dynamo windows. The map shows a slice of |\mathbf{B}| for the most unstable mode.

The measured thresholds (Rm \approx 8.87, 17.60 and 26.80) recover the known windows (8.9 < Rm < 17.5, then Rm \gtrsim 27). This computation serves as a 3D test bed for the PhD: generating eigenmodes conditioned on Rm from a vector potential.

To go further: module p3 — MHD and the dynamo effect and experiment E3.
